My daughter wants to learn Croatian. Duolingo did not offer it when she looked, so the obvious answer was: build the app myself. The same week I read an article about dark factories, software production lines where agents write and verify the code and the human appears only at the gates. Two ideas clicked together, and I decided to test the concept on a real product instead of a toy.

Six days after reading that article, on November 2, 2026, this was the situation when I screenshotted the board that afternoon: a working local-first Croatian learning app with a four-screen lesson loop, spaced repetition, family profiles, generated audio and illustrations. Behind it a Trello board where 21 cards had merged across the factory's products, 11 more were waiting on real dependency chains, three one-shot AI workers were building in parallel, and two changes sat parked in a column that exists purely for me to click accept on. The board software itself is at version 20. Many of those versions encode a lesson, and most of the lessons came from something that broke.

This is the story of both: the factory, and the thing the factory is producing.

## What a dark factory actually means here

The article I read was "What Is a Software Factory? AI and Dark Factories Explained", a Signadot guide by Arjun Iyer from October 2026. It describes a spectrum. At one end, AI is autocomplete. At the other end, the dark factory: specs go in, working software comes out, and no human writes or reviews code, the software equivalent of lights-out manufacturing. The most cited example in the piece is a three-person company, StrongDM, that lives by two rules, "code must not be written by humans" and "code must not be reviewed by humans", backed by a test universe so fast and faithful that validation replaces review.

What stayed with me was not the hype but the warning in the middle of the article. A factory that scales up generation without scaling up verification builds an unstable control loop: more effort, more incidents, less output. And the useful question is not "should my whole factory go dark", it is "which change classes have a sensor I trust". That question is the design brief for everything below.

My setup sits deliberately in the middle of the spectrum. Agents build while I am doing something else, often while I sleep. But I appear at the gates: I approve specs, I merge the pull requests that need judgment (a written policy merges the low-risk ones), and I test the deployed result myself before a card counts as done. The agents do the keyboard work; the approvals stay with me.

## The product

OpenLingo is a local-first language app, pilot language Croatian. It is a PWA: Next.js, service worker pre-caching, IndexedDB through Dexie, Howler for audio, and the FSRS spaced repetition algorithm per family profile. No accounts, no backend; progress lives in the browser.

The method borrows from Fluent Forever: zero translation friction during lessons. Words arrive as image plus native audio; Dutch labels exist only as optional help text per profile, never as the front of a card. A three-second co-creation step lets the learner pick which of three pictures sticks in memory, and a sentence slot machine lets a child assemble silly Croatian sentences from who, action and what. Grammar lands through visual minimal pairs, not through case tables. One set of exercises serves everyone: audio, big stickers and color-coded word blocks that work for a four-year-old and an adult alike, with each profile advancing at its own pace through the spaced repetition schedule.

The MVP target is concrete: about 650 picturable words in roughly 22 units, a Unit 0 of twelve greeting chunks so a learner can say *Bok! Kako si?* on day one, a small grammar core that gets a person talking, backup and restore, and an agent-driven content queue that fills the course in a few days. On November 2 the app engine exists, the plan is approved, and the content is still queued.

## The design that made it survivable

I run Hermes Agent, an open-source agent platform by Nous Research, as the orchestrator, on GLM 5. Reviewing is done by other model families through a separate CLI, so the author model never judges its own work. The pieces that make this survivable:

**Spec first, no spec no run.** Every task starts with a numbered acceptance criteria file. The verification suite refuses to run without one. This one rule changes everything downstream: reviews argue against numbered criteria instead of taste, and a worker cannot wander off.

**A frozen sensor suite.** Before any feature work, the first task of the whole project built the verification pipeline itself: a fast iteration check and a full gate, both writing machine-readable evidence reports. It guards specs, secrets, file size budgets, and, critically, its own integrity. Any diff touching the sensors, the guards, or existing tests escalates to a human path. The suite was introduced through its own reviewed pull request, then frozen. Five days into building, it is still catching real things: an attempt to archive card files under a guarded directory tripped the path guard immediately, and a fixture change that would have made one sensor test meaningless required a full meta task to fix properly.

**Blind cross-AI reviews.** A reviewer session is fresh and stateless. It sees exactly four things: the spec, the plan if there is one, the complete diff, and the verification report. It never sees the author's reasoning, the chat history, or previous rounds. Output is a strict rubric per acceptance criterion plus a defect list with severity, file and line. No scores out of ten, because numbers invite gaming. Routine changes go to Gemini 4 Argon at high reasoning effort; sensor changes, safety-critical classes and final verdicts escalate to Claude Sonnet; architecture plans go to Claude Opus. If the author model and the reviewer disagree for two rounds, a third model family breaks the tie.

**A manual browser pass by a third session.** Automated checks cannot feel tap latency or notice that audio arrives late. For UI changes, a separate agent session drives the production build like a user, real taps, real audio, and returns findings that ride along in the review evidence.

**One dispatcher, one-shot workers, hard caps.** A cheap five-minute tick reads the board, never writes code, claims at most one card per tick and detaches a fresh worker session for it, each in its own git worktree. Max three workers building in parallel. Workers are one-shot: they run exactly one card, spec to pull request, and stop. Recovery, when a worker crashes, is a new worker, not a resume.

**Business acceptance stays with me.** Machine-verified is not accepted. A merged pull request lands the card in a BAT column, business acceptance testing, with the freshly built app deployed for me to try on a real device. My verdict is a card comment: accept, or findings verbatim back to the factory. The next cards in a dependency chain do not unlock until a blocker passes BAT.

## The board is the factory

The Trello board is not a mirror of the process, it is the process. Columns, left to right: Inbox, To Refine, Spec Approved, Implementation, Review and Release, READY_FOR_FILIP, BAT, Merged, Escaped. State transitions are derived from artifacts, verification reports, review verdicts, actual merges on the forge. When I do drag a card by hand, the system treats it as a decision and respects it; otherwise the board CLI is the only thing allowed to touch the board.

Rough cards go into To Refine, where a blind refiner session turns a one-line wish into a factory-ready spec, which a second blind session then reviews. A card carries its dependencies as real board identifiers, and the dispatcher never picks a blocked card. When a card's work finishes, it moves toward me only on evidence. Outside the auto-merge policy I read the diff and click merge; either way I test the deployed app and post my verdict. The Escaped column exists for the day a defect slips through to production: it becomes a new card and a new held-out test scenario, not a blame session.

## Evolution toward a stable board

The board software started as a thin CLI and is now at version 20, with 616 tests of its own. It is the one custom piece in this stack: glue code around the Trello API that grew into the dispatcher protocol, written with Hermes and reviewed through the same factory gates as everything else. I did not design twenty versions up front. Most of the version bumps below fix something that broke, and the interesting part of this whole experiment is how conventional the failures were.

**v2, invisible workers.** The first dispatcher design had workers move their card to the review column before the blind review ran. When a worker crashed mid-review, its card sat in a column the recovery scan never looked at: invisible, unclaimed, uncounted against the cap. A blind review of the dispatcher itself returned seventeen defects, five of them blockers. The fix redefined the protocol: a card stays in Implementation for its entire life until a review approves it, and the orphan scan now covers every state a card can be in. Crashed workers are redispatched, capped at three attempts, then parked for a human decision.

**v3, the tick that deadlocked itself.** The first tick stopped entirely when the implementation column was full, which meant three dead cards could freeze the whole queue forever, including the recovery that would have unfrozen them. Full caps now block only new dispatches; bookkeeping always runs. The same review caught a blocked-card tracker that compared against the oldest status comment instead of the newest, which would have posted 288 duplicate comments per day, and an attempts counter that never reset after my interventions, which would have made a card unrestartable.

**v5, the refiner.** Raw wishes need shaping before they are specs. To Refine became a real stage: a blind refiner session drafts the acceptance criteria, a second blind session reviews the refinement, and only approved specs reach the queue. The price is real: every raw idea now costs a full blind review cycle before it becomes a card, and I pay it because a worker that starts from a vague wish builds a vague feature. One refiner exhausted its three attempts and parked its card for me; my verdict was a single "resume" comment that re-armed it with the findings as pickup context.

**v7, the column that exists because of my own question.** After the first pull request auto-synced to Merged, I asked the obvious thing: wait, I wanted to test this first. Machine verification had passed, my acceptance had not happened. The BAT column was born that afternoon: merged code deploys automatically, I test it on the deployed app, my comment verdict moves it to Merged or kicks it back with findings, and a blocker only satisfies its dependents once it survives BAT. The trade-off is deliberate: BAT makes me the throughput limit of my own factory, and on November 2 two cards were sitting there waiting for my verdict.

**v10, one classification, no ambiguity.** Verdicts are just card comments, and comments are messy text. The fix is a single classification function that decides exactly once what every comment means, accept, kickback, neutral, or human reset, and every consumer derives from that one decision. "No" negations near an accept keyword count as rejections. Before the single classifier existed, the verdict parser and the attempts counter could read the same comment differently. The residual risk is now concentrated: a misclassified comment type is wrong everywhere at once, and every new comment form needs an entry in that one function.

**v11 and v12, deploy as a first-class concern.** Acceptance testing needs the app running. A stale server process holding the deployment port blocked the first BAT deploys, so deploys became idempotent with a fallback kill, and the tick retries a failed deploy every cycle until it succeeds. The fallback kill is deliberately scoped to that one port: the deploy step can now stop a process it did not start, and I do not want that power anywhere else.

**v14, auto-merge as a written policy.** Low-risk cards with fully green sensors now merge themselves, but only inside a policy encoded as a pure verdict: title exceptions, sensor and factory changes, path-guard escalations and the factory repo itself are always excluded. The policy, not a model, makes the decision, which is exactly what exposed the next defect.

**v15 and v16, cards that arrive without a project.** A card without a project label used to be dropped from the queue silently, and a card parked with a human-decision marker could sit at the top and starve the cards behind it. Now labels are derived live from the card every tick, unlabelled cards surface in a visible list instead of disappearing, and the tick links them itself. An unlabeled card never blocks the queue again.

**v17, unreachable success.** The auto-merge policy required "sensors green", but one pre-declared, always-failing sensor self-test made that state formally unreachable for the product repo. Workers responded rationally: they declined every auto-merge. The sensor rule became deterministic: every verification step passes, with precisely the one declared exception, which the worker must verify and quote itself. Dead code that made a policy impossible is a failure mode I did not expect to enjoy finding.

**v18, the archive that tripped its own alarm.** Full specifications outgrew card descriptions, so refined specs now export as markdown into the product repo and the card keeps a compact pointer. The export landed under a guarded directory and the product repo's own path guard flagged all eighteen files for escalation. I let the sensor win and moved the archive: one extra move, not a bypass. The guards work on their own maintainer, not just on feature work.

**v19 and v20, strictness and small frictions.** The chronic self-test exception was abolished by making the fixture task-independent, so the sensors-OK definition is now literally strict: a failing self-test blocks the merge gate, with no carve-outs left. And the markdown links on cards became full URLs, because relative paths are not clickable in Trello. Not every lesson is dramatic; some are just operator frictions.

**The snapshot rule.** One evening I dragged a card myself, by hand, eight seconds after the tick took its snapshot. The tick noticed the impossible state, verified from the mutation log that the only actor it could have been was me, and respected the drag: no revert, no worker burned on it. A factory that runs while you sleep must never undo the one manual thing its owner did.

**Told to disagree.** When the escalation reviewer's quota bucket ran dry mid-week, the mandatory check fell back to a second fresh Gemini 4 Argon session, the same family that had already reviewed the change three times. This one was the exception to the blind rule: it was shown the earlier verdict and told to attack it, which is a weaker guarantee than a reviewer that sees nothing from earlier rounds. The review artifact is labeled as a fallback, because the independence of a second model family is a real thing to lose. The lesson generalizes past the quota: a second review that is never told to disagree, or that shares context with the first, is not a second review.

## The system gates itself now

The proof point arrived on November 2. The MVP planning itself went through the factory like any code change: a planning pull request with the MVP documents, fourteen fully specified factory cards with acceptance criteria and evidence mechanisms, and a frequency proposal for the 650-word course. The blind review kicked it back with findings, dependency mistakes in the card set, an unpinned enum, a wrong unit estimate, unresolved decisions; a second round fixed them; then it merged.

That planning card was also the gate. It blocked all fourteen MVP cards by real identifier, so nothing could start until the plan itself was accepted. I merged the pull request and posted my acceptance on the card. Within the hour the dispatcher had claimed the three cards whose dependencies were satisfied, language leak fixes, the wordlist pipeline, chunk words for Unit 0, and detached three fresh workers, one per card, each in its own worktree. The board hit its implementation cap and stopped dispatching, correctly. Eleven cards were still waiting behind chains: the multi-unit pack needs the leak fixes and the chunk words; unit unlocking needs the pack; the concept registry needs the wordlist. Two of the older cards were sitting in BAT waiting for my verdict; I gave one its acceptance that same afternoon, and by the time I finished this post the merged count had already moved again.

That is the moment the experiment produced: the caps and the dependency chains did the stopping, not me.

## What the first five days of building cost

Reviews are not one-pass. Typical changes take two to four blind rounds before approval; the sensor hardening task took six. The defect lists are usually right, occasionally pedantic, and the kickback mechanism means every round costs real wall-clock time.

Quotas are a physical constraint. Buckets run dry, fallbacks fire, and a fallback review is labeled as such because the independence guarantee weakens. The routing table with its escalation rules exists precisely so that nothing switches models silently.

Agents fail in ordinary ways. One worker crashed mid-review and was redispatched as attempt two. Two parallel workers collided on a test-server port and the second waited for the first to release it. A refiner exhausted its three attempts and parked its card for me. None of these were exotic; all of them were recoverable, which is the actual design goal. Recovery is a first-class feature, not an exception handler.

The MVP is not finished. The engine, the four screens, the spaced repetition loop, profiles, generated audio and illustrations for Unit 1 all exist and shipped after their review rounds. The full course content does not exist yet: the wordlist pipeline, the concept registry and Unit 0 are cards in the factory queue, mid-build or waiting on their dependencies, and the content queue that will produce the remaining units is itself a specified card, designed with its own cron and orphan recovery like everything else in this system. When people picture a dark factory they picture generation; in practice the bottleneck is everything around it.

And the privacy footnote that matters: the orchestrator model runs through Ollama Cloud, not on my hardware, and course audio and images are generated through a paid API. Data goes to remote GPUs. I wrote about that trade-off, including the zero-logging defaults and the honest limits, in [an earlier post](/posts/glm-5.2-ollama-cloud-privacy).

## What this is and isn't

This is not "AI replaced a development team". What it did is compress a multi-person effort into something one person can run, and even that sentence needs its asterisk: run means maintain the gates, make the merge and acceptance calls, write the occasional rule that the agents cannot infer, and keep the sensors honest. The tools are not mine; I did not build the agent platform or the models, I configure and orchestrate them. The part that transfers is judgment: when a review finding is a real bug versus noise, when a card's spec is secretly two cards, when an exception in the sensor suite is a precedent and when it is a hole.

OpenLingo is one family's language app. No accounts, no telemetry, no store listing. Its roadmap runs toward more languages on the same language-agnostic engine, and my daughter's Croatian is the first acceptance test that actually matters.

The experiment continues. Version 21 of the board was being built by the factory itself while I wrote this.
